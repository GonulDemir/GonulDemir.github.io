Engineering Notes on a 1–30 MHz, 1.3 kW Solid-State SP2T RF Switch — Part II

A series–shunt SiC MOSFET SP2T RF switch study covering off-state coupling, thermal design, shunt isolation, and model limitations.

October 25, 2026 • 9 min read

Series–shunt architecture, parasitic coupling, and thermal design

Design target: 1–30 MHz · 1.3 kW · 50 Ω · SiC MOSFETs

For context, a measured series–shunt PIN-diode SPDT switch has been reported for 5–100 MHz clock applications [5]. A separate 1.5 kW study demonstrated a tunable impedance-matching network centered around 13.56 MHz [6]. These works provide relevant topology and power-frequency context, but use different devices and functions; they do not validate the SiC MOSFET switch or the estimates presented here.

This article presents calculated circuit models and design estimates. The reported RF and thermal figures are not measurements from a completed prototype.

1. From Part I to the revised design

Part I introduced a solid-state SP2T switch using two source-to-source SiC MOSFETs in each branch. Its low on-resistance kept conduction loss small, but the off-state output capacitance coupled appreciable RF power into the unselected branch as frequency increased.

For a 1.3 kW signal in a 50 Ω system, the nominal voltage and current are

VRMS = √(PZ0) = 254.95 V; IRMS = √(P/Z0) = 5.10 A.

The square root in the current expression is essential. Using the device and model assumptions documented in Appendix A, the first design’s estimated power distribution is:

Frequency Selected-port power Unselected-port leakage MOSFET conduction loss Reflected power
1 MHz 1298.31 W 0.14 W 1.51 W 0.04 W
5 MHz 1294.02 W 3.58 W 1.50 W 0.89 W
10 MHz 1280.94 W 14.07 W 1.49 W 3.51 W
30 MHz 1166.25 W 105.97 W 1.35 W 26.43 W

Values are rounded independently from the workbook’s unrounded calculations, so displayed columns can differ from 1300 W by 0.01 W. This simplified model omits explicit parasitic inductance and does not represent measured switch performance. In the model, the off-state capacitance provides a frequency-dependent path to the unselected 50 Ω load; the resulting leakage rises sharply with frequency.

2. Series MOSFET trade-off

The revised design reduces off-state capacitance while keeping conduction loss within a practical range. Candidate comparison in Appendix B uses datasheet values and a simplified two-device series model. The selected IMZC120R078M2H has a listed typical on-resistance of 78 mΩ at 25 °C and an output capacitance of 28 pF under the datasheet’s specified test conditions [1]. These capacitance values are not directly comparable across all candidates unless their test conditions match.

In the same series-only model at 30 MHz, replacing the first design’s 67 pF device assumption with 28 pF reduces estimated leakage from 105.97 W to 21.85 W. That is about a 6.9 dB reduction in this model. The estimated nominal conduction loss for the two-device series path increases from 1.51 W to 4.06 W at 25 °C. The change illustrates the design trade-off: lower capacitance improves isolation, while higher on-resistance increases heat.

The capacitance figure is a datasheet measurement at its stated bias and frequency, not a fixed capacitance valid at every RF voltage. The comparison is therefore a first-order screening tool rather than a large-signal prediction.

3. Electrothermal sizing of the series path

At the 5.10 A RMS design current, the per-device conduction estimate is

Pcond = IRMS2 × RDS(on).

For the selected device, the device datasheet gives 78 mΩ typical at 25 °C, 159 mΩ typical at 150 °C, and 204 mΩ maximum at 150 °C [1]. The conservative estimate uses the 150 °C maximum value and a 25% engineering margin:

RθJA,max = (100 − 55) / 6.63 ≈ 6.79 K/W.

The back-to-back series pair is therefore budgeted at 13.26 W for thermal sizing. With 55 °C ambient and a 100 °C target junction temperature, the required junction-to-ambient thermal resistance per device is

PFET,design = 5.102 × 0.204 × 1.25 ≈ 6.63 W.

An illustrative uncooled value of 15 K/W would imply approximately 154.5 °C junction temperature, above the target. The model therefore motivates a heatsink and an electrically insulating thermal interface. The SOFTTHERM 86/238 catalogue lists a relative permittivity of 1.9 and thermal conductivity of 2.0 W/(m·K) [3]. For this model, the 2 mm thickness and 475.55 mm² overlapping area are geometric assumptions; they yield approximately 2.10 K/W case-to-sink resistance and 4.00 pF capacitance. With a shared heatsink-to-ambient resistance of 1.53 K/W for the modeled Fischer SK 135 100 ME heatsink [4], the calculated series-device junction temperature is about 96.1 °C and the heatsink temperature about 75.3 °C.

These estimates have limited margin and depend on the assumed contact area, pressure, material properties, airflow, and heat distribution. They are not a substitute for measuring the assembled hardware. The pad capacitance is from drain to heatsink; its RF effect depends on heatsink grounding and layout, so it must not automatically be treated as added MOSFET output capacitance.

4. Adding a shunt path

The second design adds a shunt MOSFET pair to each branch. When a branch is selected, its series pair is on and its shunt pair is off. In the unselected branch, the series pair is off and the shunt pair is on, providing a low-impedance path that diverts coupled RF energy away from the inactive output.

Figure 1. Conceptual selected and unselected branch states. The diagram is illustrative; it does not show gate-drive, bias, or parasitic details.

The shunt device considered is the ST STD2N95K5, a 950 V MOSFET [2]. The summary model uses a 4.2 Ω typical on-resistance per device and a two-device back-to-back path. The simplified pair is represented by 8.4 Ω on-state resistance and approximately 4.5 pF off-state capacitance. These are model inputs, not extracted large-signal RF parameters.

Figure 2. Simplified leakage path and shunt diversion in the unselected branch.

5. Series–shunt model results

At 30 MHz, with the selected branch on and the unselected branch configured with the shunt path, Appendix C reports the following 1.3 kW power balance:

Quantity Estimated power
Selected-port load 1272.165 W
Unselected-port leakage 0.767 W
Series-pair conduction loss 8.106 W
Shunt-pair dissipation 4.564 W
Reflected power 14.399 W
Total 1300.000 W

Values are rounded independently; the total is calculated from unrounded values.

Against the 18 pF series-only case in the same model, leakage falls from 35.36 W to 0.767 W, a ratio of about 46:1 or 16.6 dB. The 105.97 W result from the Part I device/model basis gives a larger cross-model comparison, but it changes both the device and model assumptions; it should not be presented as the isolated effect of adding the shunt pair.

The 18 pF case includes an assumed contribution from the thermal interface and layout. The physical coupling depends on whether the heatsink is RF-grounded or floating, PCB geometry, and parasitics absent from the simplified model. Figure 2 and the workbook are explanatory models, not measured isolation data.

6. Shunt-device thermal constraint

The model assigns 4.564 W to the shunt pair, or about 2.282 W per MOSFET. Using the datasheet reference junction-to-ambient resistance of 50 K/W [2] gives a rough junction temperature of

TJ ≈ 55 + 2.282 × 50 = 169.1 °C.

This exceeds the 150 °C maximum junction-temperature reference used in the analysis and the 100 °C design target. The reference value is board-dependent and cannot predict the final PCB temperature, but it clearly shows that the shunt devices’ thermal path is not yet demonstrated. At a 100 °C target, the required junction-to-ambient resistance would be about 19.7 K/W or lower per device at the modeled dissipation. PCB copper, thermal vias, device mounting, and RF behavior must be designed and validated together before claiming a thermally complete design.

7. Limitations and next validation steps

The attached workbooks document the assumptions and calculations. Important limits are:

  • The RF network is simplified and omits some package, PCB, connector, gate-loop, and interconnect inductances.
  • Datasheet capacitances depend on bias, frequency, and measurement method; they are not constant under a high-power RF waveform.
  • The pad-to-heatsink capacitance is a geometric estimate. Its RF contribution depends on the heatsink’s electrical reference and physical layout.
  • Thermal calculations use estimated losses and idealized thermal resistances. They do not include measured contact resistance or a validated board-level thermal model.
  • No prototype measurement is claimed. S-parameters, power handling, temperature, switching transients, and stability remain to be measured over the intended frequency and power range.

The next engineering step is to build the layout and verify it with low-power VNA measurements, followed by staged power testing with temperature monitoring and appropriate RF safety controls. The switching sequence and gate-drive implementation also need validation for safe transitions between branches.

Conclusion

The series-only design provides low conduction loss but its modeled off-state coupling becomes significant at the upper end of the 1–30 MHz range. Selecting a lower-capacitance series MOSFET improves the modeled isolation at the cost of greater conduction loss. Adding a shunt pair reduces modeled 30 MHz leakage further, while introducing additional dissipation and a demanding thermal requirement in the unselected branch.

The series–shunt architecture is therefore a promising design direction, not a validated 1.3 kW hardware result. The RF and thermal predictions should be treated as design estimates until the assembled switch is measured.
