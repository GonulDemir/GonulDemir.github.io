Engineering Notes on a 1–30 MHz, 1.3 kW Solid-State SP2T RF Switch — Part II

Evaluation of the first SP2T RF switch design and the development, RF analysis, and thermal considerations of a revised series–shunt SiC MOSFET design.

October 25, 2026 • 14 min read

Design 1 — Evaluating the first design

Part I — Our first RF switch design

In Part I, we developed a back-to-back structure using two source-to-source SiC MOSFETs in each output branch of our SP2T RF switch, which was intended to operate over 1–30 MHz at a power level of 1300 W.

The main advantage of the first design was its low conduction resistance, which allowed RF power to be transferred to the selected output with relatively low loss. However, our calculations showed that the parasitic capacitances of the MOSFETs in the OFF state significantly limited output isolation as the operating frequency increased.

Distribution of 1300 W RF power

The output capacitance (Coss) of the Infineon IMZC140R029M2H MOSFETs used in the first design coupled RF energy to the unselected output even when the MOSFETs were off.

To evaluate how this effect changes with frequency, we created a simplified equivalent-circuit model using an input power of 1300 W and a system impedance of 50 Ω.

Power distribution 1 MHz 5 MHz 10 MHz 30 MHz
Power delivered to selected output 1298.31 W 1294.02 W 1280.94 W 1166.25 W
Power leaking to unselected output 0.14 W 3.58 W 14.07 W 105.97 W
Power dissipated in MOSFETs 1.51 W 1.50 W 1.50 W 1.35 W
Power reflected to source 0.04 W 0.89 W 3.50 W 26.43 W
Total 1300 W 1300 W 1300 W 1300 W

The values in this table come from a simplified circuit model using MOSFET datasheet parameters; they do not represent measured RF performance. Parasitic inductances are not separately defined in the model.

What changes as frequency increases?

Power delivered to the selected output. At low frequencies, nearly all input power reaches the intended output. At 30 MHz, this value falls to approximately 1166 W.

Power leaking to the unselected output. The parasitic capacitances of the OFF-state MOSFETs create an unwanted signal path through the inactive RF branch. Capacitive reactance decreases as frequency increases:

XC = 1 / (2πfC)

As a result, power transferred to the unselected output rises from only 0.14 W at 1 MHz to approximately 106 W at 30 MHz. This is the main limitation of the first design.

Power dissipated in the MOSFETs. Because of the low RDS(on), the calculated conduction losses remain around 1.5 W. This value includes only the resistive losses defined in the model.

Power reflected to the source. The frequency-dependent effect of parasitic capacitance moves the RF switch input impedance away from the nominal 50 Ω, causing an impedance mismatch. Reflected power is expressed using the input reflection coefficient (Γ):

Prefl = Pin |Γ|2

In our calculation workbook, we also use the power balance:

Prefl = Pin − Pselected − Punselected − PMOSFET losses

For example, at 30 MHz:

1300 − 1166.25 − 105.97 − 1.35 ≈ 26.43 W

This is RF power returned to the source because of impedance mismatch in the model, not heat dissipated in the MOSFETs.

Why did we change the design?

The approximately 106 W of leakage at 30 MHz in the first design showed that capacitive coupling in the OFF state was the main issue to address. We therefore added shunt elements to the series MOSFETs in the second design.

Component parameters, calculation methods, and the 1–30 MHz frequency analysis are provided in the accompanying workbook, Appendix A.

Design 2 — Series MOSFET selection and electrothermal design

We began developing the second design by selecting MOSFETs to reduce the capacitive-coupling problem identified in the first design. The main goal was to reduce OFF-state output capacitance (Coss) while keeping losses in the conduction path carrying 1300 W of RF power at an acceptable level.

There is an important trade-off between these goals. Among the MOSFETs considered, lower Coss values generally came with higher RDS(on) values. Selecting the component with the lowest capacitance alone would therefore not be appropriate for our high-power application.

MOSFET candidate evaluation

To improve RF switch performance, we compared different SiC MOSFET candidates by their OFF-state output capacitance (Coss) and on-resistance (RDS(on)). The objective was to reduce power coupled to the unselected RF output while keeping conduction losses acceptable.

Conduction loss here means the total power dissipated as heat in the RDS(on) resistances of the two series MOSFETs when they are on (P = I²R). Leakage power is the RF power transferred to the 50 Ω load on the unselected output while the MOSFETs are off.

Table 1. Comparison of MOSFET candidates by conduction loss and estimated 30 MHz capacitive leakage power. Conduction losses use 25°C RDS(on) values. Leakage power is estimated with a simplified circuit model using a 1300 W available source, 50 Ω source and load impedances, two series MOSFETs, and no shunt path. Coss values depend on the datasheet measurement conditions and may not represent the same VDS for every candidate.

Based on this evaluation, we selected the Infineon IMZC120R078M2H. In the same simplified model, replacing the first design’s 67 pF value with 28 pF reduces leakage to the unselected output from about 106 W to 21.85 W [1]. This predicts an isolation improvement of approximately 6.9 dB from changing the MOSFET alone, while nominal conduction loss at 25°C rises from 1.51 W to 4.06 W.

This result shows that lower capacitance benefits RF isolation, but it must be evaluated together with conduction loss and thermal requirements. Because the selected MOSFET’s 78 mΩ on-resistance increases with temperature, we next performed an electrothermal analysis to assess the heatsink requirement.

Candidate data and calculation formulas are provided in Appendix B.

Electrothermal design and heatsink selection

Temperature-dependent conduction losses

In addition to the trade-off between Coss and RDS(on), we considered how on-resistance changes with temperature. The increase in RDS(on) of the selected Infineon IMZC120R078M2H with junction temperature also increases conduction losses.

For 1300 W RF power in a 50 Ω system, the nominal current is:

IRMS = √(1300/50) = 5.10 A

Conduction loss per MOSFET is calculated as:

Pcond = IRMS2 × RDS(on)
Operating condition RDS(on) Loss per MOSFET
25°C, typical 78 mΩ 2.03 W
150°C, typical 159 mΩ 4.13 W
150°C, maximum 204 mΩ 5.30 W

A 25% engineering margin was added to the 5.30 W loss calculated using the maximum RDS(on) at 150°C. This gives 6.63 W per MOSFET and a thermal design power of 13.26 W for the conducting back-to-back pair. This is a conservative sizing case that accounts for the increase in resistance with temperature.

Determining the cooling requirement

For an ambient temperature of 55°C and a target junction temperature of 100°C, the maximum permitted total junction-to-ambient thermal resistance is:

RθJA,max = (Tj,max − Ta)/PFET = (100 − 55)/6.63 = 6.79 K/W

Without a heatsink, junction temperature depends on total thermal resistance. For example, assuming 15 K/W gives an estimated junction temperature of approximately 154.5°C. This is a comparison scenario to evaluate the cooling requirement, not a verified RθJA result for the selected MOSFET.

A separate heatsink was considered to meet the 100°C operating target. Mounting the MOSFETs on a shared metal heatsink also creates an electrical-isolation requirement.

Electrical insulation and parasitic capacitance

The metal mounting tab of the IMZC120R078M2H is electrically connected to its drain terminal. Directly mounting MOSFETs at different RF potentials to a common heatsink could create an unwanted electrical connection between drain nodes.

At 1300 W into a 50 Ω load, the nominal voltage is approximately 255 V RMS (361 V peak), so electrical insulation and voltage withstand are important. We therefore considered the Kerafol SOFTTHERM 86/238 insulating material [3].

The insulating layer forms a dielectric between the MOSFET’s metal drain tab and the aluminum heatsink. Although the surfaces are not electrically connected, the facing conductive surfaces create a parasitic capacitance between drain and heatsink, denoted Cpad:

Cpad = ε0 εr A/d

This capacitance does not change the MOSFET’s intrinsic Coss, but it creates an additional capacitive coupling path in the RF circuit. Depending on the electrical reference of the heatsink, this path can affect input impedance, output isolation, and common-mode currents.

Increasing the insulation thickness reduces Cpad while increasing thermal resistance:

Rθ,pad = d/(kA)

Insulation thickness therefore creates a direct trade-off between thermal management and RF performance.

Using approximately 1.9 relative permittivity for SOFTTHERM 86/238 and a modeled effective overlap area of 475.55 mm², a 2 mm insulation thickness gives an estimated Cpad of approximately 4.00 pF. The Cseries,OFF value of 18 pF used in the RF calculations is a simplified model input formed by adding approximately 4 pF Cpad to the ideal 14 pF series equivalent derived from two IMZC120R078M2H MOSFETs, each assumed to be 28 pF.

However, Cpad is between drain and heatsink. Because the heatsink may be connected to RF ground or left electrically floating, and because the actual circuit nodes matter, this capacitance cannot generally be treated as directly in parallel with the OFF path. Therefore, 18 pF is an assumed value for sensitivity analysis, not an equivalent capacitance validated from the physical topology. The actual equivalent must be verified by circuit simulation and measurement with the heatsink connection and PCB parasitics included.

Model limitation: The 18 pF value is not a measured or PCB-derived OFF capacitance. The estimated 0.767 W unselected-output power, 4.564 W shunt loss, and 16.6 dB isolation difference at 30 MHz apply only under the stated assumptions of 50 Ω, fixed capacitances, and zero additional inductance. Results may change with voltage-dependent Coss, the electrical connection of the heatsink, and PCB parasitics.

For the thermal model, the interface resistance for the same thickness was assumed to be approximately 2.10 K/W.

Heatsink requirement and SK 135 100 ME selection

When two series MOSFETs share a heatsink, junction temperature is modeled as:

Tj = Ta + PpairRθSA + PFET(RθJC + RθCS)

Using a 100°C target junction temperature, 55°C ambient, 6.63 W loss per MOSFET, 1.04 K/W junction-to-case resistance, and a 2.10 K/W interface resistance for 2 mm insulation, the maximum heatsink-to-ambient thermal resistance is:

RθSA,max = [100 − 55 − 6.63(1.04 + 2.10)]/13.26 ≈ 1.82 K/W

We selected the Fischer Elektronik SK 135 100 ME heatsink for this requirement. Its 1.53 K/W natural-convection thermal resistance used in the calculations is below the 1.82 K/W upper limit. Its geometry also allows both series MOSFETs to be mounted on a shared profile.

Additional thermal and electrical effects from mounting hardware (screws, washers, insulating bushings, and other fasteners) are not included. Tightening torque, contact pressure, compression of the insulator, and mounting geometry can change interface thermal resistance and parasitic capacitance. The calculated junction temperature is a preliminary design estimate that does not include these effects.

With the selected heatsink and insulation combination:

Tj = 55 + (13.26 × 1.53) + 6.63(1.04 + 2.10) ≈ 96.1 °C

The heatsink temperature is estimated at approximately 75.3°C and the MOSFET junction temperature at 96.1°C. In comparison, the assumed 15 K/W no-heatsink scenario gives 154.5°C, a reduction of approximately 58.3°C. This comparison uses an assumed reference thermal resistance rather than a verified no-heatsink value.

The reactance at 30 MHz of the approximately 4 pF capacitance associated with 2 mm insulation is:

XC = 1/[2π(30 × 106)(4 × 10−12)] ≈ 1.33 kΩ

The effect of this additional capacitance on the RF power budget was examined with a simplified circuit model containing a single equivalent Cpad:

30 MHz RF model Reflected power
Without Cpad 9.78 W
Cpad ≈ 4 pF 14.40 W
Additional reflected power 4.62 W

Thus, the approximately 4 pF Cpad associated with 2 mm insulation increases reflected power in the model by about 4.62 W. This is not thermal loss in the insulating material; it is the result of impedance mismatch caused by capacitive loading.

The Fischer SK 135 100 ME heatsink and 2 mm Kerafol SOFTTHERM 86/238 insulation were selected by considering the calculated thermal requirement and RF parasitics together. The estimated junction temperature of approximately 96°C is below the 100°C target, but the thermal margin is limited, so final performance must be verified on a prototype.

Shunt MOSFET selection and its effect on RF isolation

Even when the series MOSFETs are off, the RF path is not a complete open circuit. Coupling through OFF-state capacitance transfers RF energy to the unselected output, particularly as frequency increases.

Simplified RF calculations at 1300 W input power and 50 Ω port impedance give the following results at 30 MHz:

Series MOSFET Power at unselected output
IMZC140R029M2H 105.97 W
IMZC120R078M2H 21.85 W

Changing the MOSFET reduces leakage power by approximately 79.4%. However, the 21.85 W reaching the unselected output shows that reducing only the series MOSFET OFF capacitance is not sufficient.

We therefore added shunt MOSFETs to suppress the voltage in the unselected RF branch [5], [6].

Shunt MOSFET selection criteria

The operating conditions for the shunt MOSFETs differ from those for the series MOSFETs. In the selected RF branch, the shunt devices remain off; in the unselected branch, they are turned on. Device selection must therefore account for both operating states.

The main selection criteria are:

  • Low OFF capacitance: Coss is the typical 9 pF value given in the datasheet at VDS = 100 V and f = 1 MHz. For two 9 pF MOSFETs connected back-to-back, an approximate pair equivalent of 4.5 pF is used as a simplified linear model. Voltage-dependent capacitance and RF swing at 1300 W must be verified on the prototype.
  • Adequate drain-source voltage rating: withstand voltages that may occur during 1300 W RF operation, including reflection and transient conditions.
  • Acceptable RDS(on): create a low-impedance path that suppresses voltage at the unselected output while limiting losses in that path.

These criteria involve a trade-off. High-voltage MOSFETs with low capacitance generally have higher on-resistance. Selection therefore considers the combined RF effect of OFF capacitance and ON resistance, rather than only the lowest RDS(on).

We selected the STMicroelectronics STD2N95K5. Its 950 V drain-source rating was an important criterion for evaluation in a high-power RF application. The low output capacitance is intended to limit loading of the active RF path, while the 4.2 Ω on-resistance used in the model at 25°C determines the impedance of the shunt branch when on [2].

Two MOSFETs are connected back-to-back to provide blocking for both RF voltage polarities. The total ON resistance is:

Rshunt,ON = 2RDS(on) = 2 × 4.2 = 8.4 Ω

This does not create an ideal short circuit. It provides an additional path for RF current coupled into the unselected branch through the series MOSFET OFF capacitance, helping to reduce the output voltage.

RF power distribution and isolation improvement

The effect of the series–shunt topology was evaluated with a simplified RF circuit model at 1300 W input power and 50 Ω port impedance [6]. Figure 1 shows the series and shunt switch states of the selected and unselected branches at the equivalent-model level.

Unselected output power 0.00087 W 0.344 W 0.767 W
Shunt MOSFET loss 0.0052 W 2.046 W 4.564 W

In the updated 18 pF OFF-capacitance model, coupling increases with frequency. At 30 MHz, 0.767 W reaches the unselected load while 4.564 W is dissipated in the shunt MOSFET pair. With the same assumed 18 pF series OFF capacitance, the updated model without a shunt path gives 35.36 W leakage at 30 MHz. The 21.85 W result from the earlier candidate comparison uses different capacitance and model assumptions and is not used in this ratio:

35.36/0.767 ≈ 46.1
ΔI = 10 log10(35.36/0.767) ≈ 16.6 dB

Figure 2 compares the power reaching the unselected output with and without the shunt branch under the same updated model assumptions.

Figure 2. Calculated power reaching the unselected 50 Ω load at 30 MHz. The model assumes Cseries = 18 pF, Cshunt = 4.5 pF, and Rshunt = 8.4 Ω. These are model results, not measurements.

Compared with the first design’s 105.97 W leakage, the overall improvement calculated for the second design is:

ΔItotal = 10 log10(105.97/0.767) ≈ 21.4 dB

This 21.4 dB value compares the 105.97 W result from the first design with the 0.767 W result from the second design. Because the two results use different MOSFET selections and equivalent-capacitance/model assumptions, this must not be interpreted as the effect of adding the shunt pair alone. The 16.6 dB calculation above isolates the shunt effect using the same 18 pF model. Neither result is a measured isolation value.

The 4.564 W loss calculated in the shunt MOSFETs at 30 MHz is different from the 0.767 W delivered to the unselected load. The shunt branch dissipates part of the RF energy in its ON resistance, suppressing the output voltage. The improvement in isolation therefore comes with additional thermal loss in the shunt devices.

In summary, the second design reduces RF coupling with the low OFF capacitance of the series MOSFET and suppresses the unselected output voltage more effectively by adding shunt MOSFETs. The calculated isolation values are based on simplified models. In particular, the comparison between 35.36 W and 0.767 W under the same updated model must be validated with voltage-dependent Coss, actual MOSFET RF impedance, PCB parasitics, and common return paths taken into account.

Thermal condition of the shunt MOSFETs

The shunt MOSFET thermal assessment uses 30 MHz, the highest operating frequency. In the preceding RF model, the total loss caused by current coupled into the unselected output and conducted through the shunt MOSFET ON resistance is 4.564 W:

Pshunt = Ishunt2 Rshunt,ON
4.564 = Ishunt2 × 8.4; Ishunt,RMS = √(4.564/8.4) ≈ 0.737 A

Assuming both back-to-back MOSFETs carry the same current and each has a 4.2 Ω on-resistance:

PFET = 0.7372 × 4.2 ≈ 2.282 W

Thus, 4.564 W is the total loss for the pair, while 2.282 W is the thermal load per MOSFET. This calculation uses the 25°C on-resistance used in the RF model.

Using the datasheet value RθJA = 50 K/W for a 1 in² FR-4 board with 2 oz copper, the first-order junction-temperature estimate for each MOSFET at 2.282 W loss and 55°C ambient is [2]:

Tj = Ta + PFETRθJA ≈ 55 + 2.282 × 50 = 169.1 °C

This approximate value exceeds the 150°C maximum junction temperature. The calculation uses the 25°C RDS(on), so it is optimistic; higher resistance at the actual temperature could increase loss and temperature. The datasheet RθJA applies only under the stated standard test-board conditions. The actual PCB requires measurement or thermal analysis.

Thermal design decision: The 169.1°C value is a first-order estimate based on 25°C typical RDS(on) and RθJA for a specified reference PCB. It does not include self-heating effects on RDS(on), so it must not be treated as the final operating temperature. It shows that the adequacy of cooling through the current PCB has not yet been demonstrated. Shunt MOSFET junction temperature, copper/via geometry, and RF isolation must be verified together on a prototype before thermal suitability is claimed.

To meet a 100°C target, the maximum required thermal resistance is:

RθJA,max = (100 − 55)/2.282 ≈ 19.7 K/W

More effective heat spreading is therefore needed relative to the reference-board condition.

As an initial approach, cooling through the PCB was considered instead of an external heatsink. However, because the metal tab of the DPAK package is connected to drain, the same thermal solution cannot be used for all four shunt MOSFETs.

In the circuit topology, the drains of Q1 and Q6 are connected to common ground, while the drains of Q2 and Q5 are connected to RF_OUT_A and RF_OUT_B. Thermal vias and copper areas connected to the ground plane can therefore be used for Q1 and Q6, but the drain pads of Q2 and Q5 cannot be connected to ground.

Increasing the copper pad area for Q2 and Q5 may help spread heat into the PCB. However, this area connected to the RF output creates parasitic capacitance with the ground plane directly below it:

Cpar ≈ ε0εrA/d

Here, A is the overlap area between the RF pad and ground plane, and d is the dielectric thickness between layers. Capacitance increases approximately with copper area.

For example, assuming a dielectric thickness of 0.65 mm and relative permittivity of 3.8, each additional 100 mm² of overlap with the RF pad can add approximately 5.2 pF:

Cpar ≈ (8.85 × 10−12 × 3.8 × 100 × 10−6)/(0.65 × 10−3) ≈ 5.2 pF

The reactance of this capacitance at 30 MHz is:

XC = 1/[2π(30 × 106)(5.2 × 10−12)] ≈ 1.02 kΩ

Although this may appear to be a high impedance on its own, the additional capacitance can change the RF-line impedance and isolation when combined with the MOSFET’s existing parasitics. We therefore do not plan to increase pad area without control solely for thermal reasons.

Similarly, if thermal vias are used for Q2 and Q5, they must pass through the ground planes without connecting to them and must join only isolated copper areas belonging to the same RF net. This approach also creates additional capacitive coupling and PCB layout constraints.

The current design approach is to spread heat through the PCB without an external heatsink for the shunt MOSFETs, use thermal vias for the MOSFETs connected to ground, and keep the copper area controlled for MOSFETs connected to the RF outputs.

This approach has not yet been validated. Junction temperatures of Q2 and Q5 and the effect of the thermal copper geometry on RF isolation will be assessed with thermal and RF measurements on a prototype. Pad dimensions, thermal-via arrangement, or the need for additional cooling will be reconsidered based on the measurement results.

Overall assessment of the second design and conclusion

In the second design, a low-Coss series MOSFET and back-to-back shunt MOSFETs give a calculated 30 MHz leakage of 0.767 W. The calculated 21.4 dB improvement over the first design depends on model assumptions and is not a measurement result.

The Kelvin-source connections of the series MOSFET pairs are also configured through a common source reference. This connection arrangement, which was raised in earlier evaluations, is retained as a design assumption for the current prototype. However, connecting the Kelvin-source terminals to a common reference does not eliminate the effects of parasitic inductance in the gate-drive loop. In particular, the physical arrangement of the high-current source path and the gate-driver return can affect the actual gate-source voltage and switching behavior. The suitability of this connection must therefore be verified by electrical measurements on the prototype [4].

The calculated junction temperature for the series MOSFET heatsink is 96.1°C. Cooling the shunt MOSFETs through the PCB and its effect on RF performance must be verified by prototype measurements.

In particular, the 4.564 W loss calculated for the shunt MOSFET pair at 30 MHz and the first-order temperature estimate of approximately 169.1°C under the reference PCB conditions are critical points that require improvement and validation. The series and shunt thermal results, as well as the RF isolation values, are not yet prototype measurements.

Final note: the design is ongoing

We are still working on the PCB design for our RF switch. We know that the success of the circuit depends greatly on the PCB layout. We have repeatedly reviewed the layout to minimize unwanted coupling between the RF power paths and the low-frequency control and gate-drive circuits, manage return currents correctly, and keep parasitic effects under control. Sometimes moving a connection by only a few millimeters sent us back to the beginning of the design.

This work is therefore not the story of a finished product; it is a record of an ongoing engineering process. I hope to share the next part of this story together with the PCB design, prototype, and measurement results.
