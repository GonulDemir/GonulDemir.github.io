*Part I — Design 1: targets, architecture and engineering assumptions*

**Authors:** Gönül Demir and [Hadrien Theveneau](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hadrien-theveneau-4a2b0883/)

## An open engineering project

This project began with a shared question: how can a solid-state switch route a high-power HF signal reliably between two outputs? The target is a 50 Ω SP2T switch covering **1–30 MHz** and designed for up to **1.3 kW**. In the KiCad schematic, the RF ports are named `RF_COMMON`, `RF_ANT_TEST` and `RF_DUMMY`.

The objective is not to present a finished product. We are publishing the project as an open engineering series: requirements, calculations, component choices, schematics, design reviews, revisions, measurements and unexpected results will remain visible as the work develops. Hadrien Theveneau will lead RF characterisation and staged power testing; I will develop the electronics and PCB, assemble the prototype and document the design decisions.

## What makes this difficult? — initial risk analysis

At 1.3 kW into a matched 50 Ω load, the nominal electrical stress is already significant:

$$
V_{RMS}=\sqrt{PR}=\sqrt{1300\times50}\approx255\ \text{V}
$$

$$
V_{PEAK}=\sqrt{2}V_{RMS}\approx361\ \text{V},\qquad I_{RMS}=\sqrt{P/R}\approx5.1\ \text{A}
$$

These are matched-load values, not worst-case values. An antenna can present a high VSWR, and mismatch can increase voltage and current beyond the nominal estimate. Before selecting components, we identified the main risks:

- **OFF-state isolation:** MOSFET output capacitance can pass RF to the unselected branch.
- **Nonlinear Coss:** capacitance and stored charge vary with voltage; one datasheet value is not a complete large-signal model.
- **Voltage sharing:** the series devices and their floating source node must share RF stress predictably.
- **RF layout:** connector launches, drain-pad area, package inductance and return paths are part of the circuit.
- **Gate-drive immunity:** high dV/dt can disturb floating references or cause false turn-on.
- **Thermal and mechanical behaviour:** the TO-247 package, insulation and heatsink can influence RF performance.
- **Measurement confidence:** the excitation path and the verification path must be independent.

For this reason, validation is staged: low-power continuity and control checks first, then VNA measurements, then controlled power increases into a suitable dummy load. Full-power antenna testing comes only after protection and mismatch behaviour are understood.

## Design 1: why back-to-back MOSFETs were selected

The first architecture uses two RF branches. Each branch contains two N-channel SiC MOSFETs connected source-to-source. A single MOSFET cannot block both polarities of an RF waveform because its intrinsic body diode provides a one-way path. In a back-to-back pair, both devices conduct bidirectionally when ON, while the opposed body diodes block both polarities when OFF.

The SP2T therefore contains four power MOSFETs:

$$
2\ \text{branches}\times2\ \text{MOSFETs per branch}=4\ \text{MOSFETs}
$$

This was an attractive starting point: it provided a compact solid-state RF path, static ON/OFF states and high voltage capability without mechanical contacts. The important question was whether the same architecture could also deliver sufficient isolation at 30 MHz and 1.3 kW.

## MOSFET selection: VDS, RDS(on) and Coss

The [Infineon IMZC140R029M2H](https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/60/49/infineon-imzc140r029m2h-datasheet-en.pdf) was selected for Design 1 because it combines a **1400 V VDS rating**, low nominal **RDS(on)**, SiC technology and a four-lead TO-247 package with a dedicated Kelvin Source terminal.

In the ON state, conduction loss can be estimated as:

$$
P_{COND}=I_{RMS}^{2}R_{DS(on)}
$$

For two devices in series, an initial room-temperature estimate of the total channel resistance was approximately 58 mΩ. At 5.1 A RMS:

$$
P_{COND}\approx5.1^{2}\times0.058\approx1.5\ \text{W}
$$

This result made Design 1 look promising. However, an RF switch is also defined by its OFF state. The MOSFET is not an ideal open circuit; Coss creates a frequency-dependent path:

$$
X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}
$$

As frequency increases, this impedance decreases. At 30 MHz, voltage-dependent Coss, stored charge, floating-node behaviour, PCB coupling and connector parasitics can produce meaningful leakage even when the DC resistance is extremely high. This became the central risk in the first design review.

## Gate-drive architecture

A 5 V MCU signal is not sufficient to drive the selected SiC MOSFET directly. The gate is a charge-driven capacitive input. The device is characterised around 54 nC total gate charge and approximately 14 nC gate-drain charge at the intended drive voltage. A first-order estimate of the required gate current is:

$$
I_G\approx Q_G/t_{SW}
$$

The [Texas Instruments UCC21550ADWR](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/ucc21550.pdf) was selected because it provides two independent isolated channels, reinforced isolation, programmable dead time, hardware disable and high peak source/sink capability. It forms a suitable boundary between logic control and the floating MOSFET domains.

The target gate states are approximately **+18 V for ON** and **−3 to −3.5 V for OFF**. The negative OFF bias improves immunity to Miller-induced false turn-on:

$$
I_{Miller}=C_{GD}\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}
$$

Gate resistance, driver output impedance and PCB inductance must be tuned together. Excessive resistance makes the transition slow and increases time in the high-dissipation region; too little resistance increases ringing, EMI and parasitic oscillation. The design goal is controlled switching, not simply the fastest edge.

### Why the QA053C-1803R3 supply was needed

The UCC21550 does not create the gate-bias rails by itself. Each floating gate-drive domain therefore uses an isolated [MORNSUN QA053C-1803R3](https://www.mornsun-power.com/public/uploads/pdf/QAxx3C-R3.pdf) converter to generate approximately +18 V and −3.5 V relative to its own Kelvin source. Independent supplies prevent the two floating domains from creating an unintended return path through logic ground.

### Why the Kelvin Source connection matters

The power Source terminal carries RF current and its parasitic inductance produces a voltage:

$$
V_{LS}=L_S\frac{di}{dt}
$$

If the driver return shares this path, the voltage drop becomes part of the gate loop and changes the actual VGS. The dedicated Kelvin Source separates the gate-reference path from the high-current Source path. In the schematic, `Kelvin_A` and `Kelvin_B` are floating gate-drive references; they are not logic ground or chassis ground.

## Logic isolation and RF-state verification

The enable, branch-select and detector signals enter through [TLP291-4 optocouplers](https://toshiba.semicon-storage.com/info/TLP291-4_datasheet_en_20191129.pdf). This prevents external cables from creating uncontrolled common-mode, ground-loop or RF-coupling paths. The isolated signals are conditioned with the [SN74HC08PWR](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/sn74hc08.pdf), providing a hardware gating layer before the UCC21550 inputs. Logic gating cannot prove RF safety, but it limits invalid combinations during startup, reset and branch transitions.

An OFF command is not proof that RF energy has disappeared from a branch. Coss, connector coupling and transient behaviour can leave substantial energy on the unselected port. An independent RF detector or calibrated power-measurement path is therefore required to verify the actual RF state before the second branch is enabled:

**Selected branch OFF → RF level verified low → second branch ON**

The same independent measurement path will later be used to evaluate forward power, reflected power, insertion loss and leakage into the unselected port. It must not be confused with the control signal that commands the switch.

## The first design review changed the direction

Design 1 was a coherent engineering baseline: a clear RF path, high-voltage SiC devices, isolated bipolar gate drive, Kelvin references, logic isolation and a staged test plan. We initially expected this architecture to be a strong candidate for the prototype.

After the first project meeting and design review with Hadrien Theveneau, we decided **not to release Design 1 to PCB fabrication**. The decision was driven by the OFF-state isolation risk, not by a failure of the overall project. A low Coss value and a low conduction-loss estimate did not fully describe the large-signal RF behaviour at 30 MHz. Voltage-dependent capacitance, charge behaviour, floating nodes, PCB coupling and connector parasitics had to be evaluated together.

Design 1 therefore became an engineering reference: it showed exactly which problem the next architecture must solve. We are now evaluating series/shunt paths, shunt MOSFET options, devices with lower OFF-state capacitance and high-power PIN-diode alternatives. The new designs are nearing completion and will be presented in the next parts of this series.

## Safety notice

This project involves potentially lethal RF voltages and high power. It is an experimental engineering study, not a certified product or construction recommendation. High-power testing requires suitable loads, shielding, interlocks, calibrated equipment and experienced supervision.
