*Part I — Design 1: targets, architecture and engineering assumptions*

**Authors:** Gönül Demir and [Hadrien Theveneau](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hadrien-theveneau-4a2b0883/)

## An open engineering series

In the KiCad schematic, the three RF ports are named `RF_COMMON`, `RF_ANT_TEST` and `RF_DUMMY`. These names are used throughout the project so that the functional diagram, schematic and test notes describe the same signal path.

We are developing an experimental solid-state SP2T RF switch for a 50 Ω HF system. The target is to route a transmitter between an antenna/test port and a dummy load across **1–30 MHz**, with a design target of **1.3 kW**.

This project is not being presented as a finished product. It is a step-by-step engineering series: design assumptions, calculations, schematics, PCB decisions, revisions, measurements and failures will be published as the work progresses. The design is open source so that engineers and radio amateurs can inspect the reasoning, reproduce the calculations and contribute useful criticism.

Hadrien Theveneau will carry out the RF characterisation and staged power tests. I will develop the electronics and PCB, assemble and bring up the prototype, verify the control and gate-drive sections, and incorporate the test results into later revisions.

## What makes this project difficult?

At first glance, the requirement looks simple: switch a 50 Ω RF signal from one output to another. At 1.3 kW, however, the matched-load voltage is already substantial:

$$
V_{RMS}=\sqrt{PR}=\sqrt{1300\times50}\approx255\ \text{V}
$$

$$
V_{PEAK}=\sqrt{2}V_{RMS}\approx361\ \text{V},\qquad I_{RMS}=\sqrt{P/R}\approx5.1\ \text{A}
$$

The design must handle this voltage and current while maintaining a useful 50 Ω path, low insertion loss and high isolation at the unselected port. The hardest problems are the interaction between nonlinear capacitance, RF layout, connector transitions, floating gate-drive domains, thermal mounting, mismatch conditions and measurement uncertainty.

### Initial risk analysis

Before committing to a PCB, we identified the main risks:

- **OFF-state isolation:** MOSFET output capacitance can provide an unintended RF path to the unselected port.
- **Nonlinear Coss behaviour:** capacitance and stored charge vary with voltage, so a small-signal datasheet number is not a complete large-signal isolation model.
- **Voltage sharing:** the two series MOSFETs and their floating common-source node must share RF stress predictably.
- **Layout parasitics:** connector launches, drain-pad area, package inductance and return-current paths become part of the RF circuit.
- **Gate-drive integrity:** RF common-mode transients can cause unintended turn-on or disturb the floating gate references.
- **Thermal and mechanical constraints:** the TO-247 drain tab, insulating hardware and heatsink capacitance can affect both safety and RF behaviour.
- **Mismatch testing:** an antenna is not a guaranteed 50 Ω load; open- and short-circuit conditions can produce stresses far above the matched-load estimate.
- **Measurement confidence:** a result can be misleading if the same detector chain is used both to drive and to judge the switch.

These risks define the validation order: low-power continuity and control checks first, then VNA measurements, then controlled power increases into a suitable dummy load.

## Design 1: why the original architecture was attractive

Design 1 uses two RF branches. Each branch contains two N-channel SiC MOSFETs connected source-to-source. When both devices are ON, the pair conducts bidirectionally. When both are OFF, the opposed body diodes block both polarities of the RF waveform.

The design therefore uses four MOSFETs in total:

$$
2\ \text{branches}\times2\ \text{MOSFETs per branch}=4\ \text{MOSFETs}
$$

The architecture was attractive because it offered a compact solid-state path, static gate states and a high voltage rating without mechanical contacts in the RF path. The selected parts and supporting circuits were chosen for specific engineering reasons.

### IMZC140R029M2H SiC MOSFET

The [Infineon IMZC140R029M2H](https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/60/49/infineon-imzc140r029m2h-datasheet-en.pdf) was selected for its combination of:

- 1400 V drain-source rating;
- low nominal ON resistance;
- dedicated Kelvin-source terminal;
- SiC switching technology suitable for high-voltage transients;
- a package and gate-charge range compatible with the intended driver.

For two devices in series, a first-order room-temperature channel resistance is approximately 58 mΩ. At 5.1 A RMS, the resistance-only conduction estimate is:

$$
P_{COND}\approx I_{RMS}^{2}\times0.058\approx1.5\ \text{W}
$$

This explains why the MOSFET looked promising at the beginning: the conduction-loss estimate is small and the voltage rating provides useful margin. It is an initial estimate, not a prediction of finished-switch performance. Temperature, package inductance, PCB loss, connector loss, mismatch and OFF-state capacitance still have to be verified.

### Why the Kelvin connection matters

The Kelvin-source connection provides a low-inductance reference for the gate driver that is separated from the high-current source path. Without this separation, RF current and common-source inductance can create an unwanted voltage between the driver reference and the actual source potential. That error directly changes the effective gate voltage and can reduce switching margin or promote parasitic turn-on.

In Design 1, `Kelvin_A` and `Kelvin_B` are floating gate-drive references. They are deliberately separate from logic ground, chassis ground and the RF current nodes.

### Why UCC21550ADWR was selected

The [Texas Instruments UCC21550](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/ucc21550.pdf) provides two isolated gate-drive channels with the isolation and timing features needed for the floating MOSFET domains. It was selected to support reinforced isolation, independent drive channels, programmable dead time and gate-drive current appropriate for charging and discharging the SiC MOSFET gates.

The driver is not a substitute for a complete interlock. A later revision should retain an independent one-hot interlock in the logic path as a second layer of protection.

The gate is a capacitive, charge-driven input rather than a static logic node. The selected SiC device is characterized around 54 nC total gate charge and 14 nC gate-drain charge at the intended drive voltage. A first estimate is:

$$
I_G\approx Q_G/t_{SW}
$$

This is why the driver, gate resistor and Kelvin return must be designed together. Excessive resistance slows the transition; too little resistance increases ringing, EMI and parasitic oscillation risk. The goal is controlled switching, not the shortest possible edge.

### Why QA053C-1803R3 isolated supplies were selected

Each floating MOSFET domain needs a local bipolar gate supply. The [MORNSUN QA053C-1803R3](https://www.mornsun-power.com/public/uploads/pdf/QAxx3C-R3.pdf) was selected to provide an isolated approximately +18 V / −3.5 V supply referenced to the corresponding Kelvin node.

The positive rail provides the intended ON bias. The negative OFF bias improves immunity to Miller-induced false turn-on when the RF drain voltage changes rapidly. Two independent converters are used so that the floating gate domains do not unintentionally share a return path through the control circuitry.

In Design 1 the nominal gate states are approximately **+18 V for ON** and **-3 to -3.5 V for OFF**, always measured from the corresponding Kelvin source. The negative rail is deliberately moderate: it adds turn-off margin without approaching the device's specified negative gate limit.

### Why SN74HC08PWR was included

The [SN74HC08PWR](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/sn74hc08.pdf) provides simple logic conditioning and gating before the isolated driver. In Design 1 it is used to combine enable/interlock conditions and ensure that an invalid command does not directly create simultaneous branch selection.

Logic gating alone cannot prove RF safety, but it creates a clear control boundary and gives the later design a place to add a hard one-hot interlock, reset behaviour and fault handling.

### Why optocouplers were considered necessary

The control side and the RF power side must not share an uncontrolled galvanic return path. TLP291-4 optocouplers provide a simple isolation boundary for the low-speed enable, branch-select and detector signals before the isolated gate-driver domains. They also make it easier to reason about what happens when the RF structure moves several hundred volts relative to the controller.

The optocoupler is not the only isolation barrier in the system; the UCC21550 and isolated gate supplies provide the power-stage isolation. The layers serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Why an independent detector is required

The switch must not be judged only by the signal that the same system uses to excite it. An independent RF detector, power meter or calibrated measurement path is needed to verify forward power, reflected power, selected-path insertion loss and leakage into the unselected port.

Independent detection helps separate a real switch response from errors caused by the source, couplers, cables, detector compression or instrument calibration. It also becomes essential during staged power testing, where a plausible-looking reading must not be mistaken for proof of safe operation.

## The first design review changed the direction

Design 1 is a coherent starting point. It has a clear signal path, a high-voltage SiC device, isolated gate drive, Kelvin references and a staged validation plan. We are documenting it in detail because the reasoning is valuable even though the design is not yet complete.

After our first project meeting and joint design review, Gönül Demir and Hadrien Theveneau concluded that the RF switching path must be redesigned before PCB release. The decision was not that the entire project had failed; it was that the isolation risk was too important to leave unresolved.

The critical issue is the large-signal effect of MOSFET output capacitance (**Coss**). The initial calculation treated capacitance as a useful first-order warning. It did not fully capture voltage-dependent charge, floating-node behaviour, device sharing, PCB coupling and connector parasitics. The new designs are now nearing completion and will be presented in the next articles.

### A preview of the next parts

The next instalments will show the revised architectures, explain why the series/shunt path and the switching device choice are being reconsidered, and compare the alternatives with updated calculations. We will publish the new schematics when that design work is complete.

Until then, the most useful contribution is a technical question: which assumption in Design 1 would you challenge first, and what measurement would you use to challenge it?

## Current status

Design 1 is an engineering baseline, not a finished or validated product. The PCB and assembled prototype have not yet demonstrated 1.3 kW operation. Hadrien Theveneau will perform RF characterisation and staged power testing after the revised architecture is ready. Measurements will determine which claims survive contact with hardware.

Technical comments are welcome, especially on Coss modelling, RF isolation, connector transitions, Kelvin routing, thermal mounting and independent measurement strategy. Please use the discussion link below to challenge the design constructively.

## Safety notice

This project involves potentially lethal RF voltages and high power. It is an experimental design, not a certified product or construction recommendation. High-power testing requires appropriate loads, shielding, interlocks, calibrated equipment and experienced supervision.
