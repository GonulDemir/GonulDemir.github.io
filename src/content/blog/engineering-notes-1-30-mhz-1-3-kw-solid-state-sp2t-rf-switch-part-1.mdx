*Part I — From the first requirement to the first design review*

**Authors:** Gönül Demir and [Hadrien Theveneau](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hadrien-theveneau-4a2b0883/)

## Why this project is worth documenting

The requirement sounds simple: select one of two RF outputs. The engineering reality is different when the switch must handle **1–30 MHz** and a target of **1.3 kW** in a 50 Ω system. The RF ports in our schematic are `RF_COMMON`, `ANTENNA_1` and `ANTENNA_2`; the switch must route the common port to one output while keeping the other output safely isolated.

This is an open engineering project, not a finished product announcement. We are publishing the reasoning behind the architecture, the calculations that supported our first choices, the assumptions that later became questionable, and the revisions that follow. Hadrien Theveneau will perform RF characterisation and staged power tests. I will develop the electronics and PCB, assemble the prototype and document the control and gate-drive behaviour.

## The electrical problem and the risk map

For a matched 50 Ω load at 1.3 kW:

$$
V_{RMS}=\sqrt{1300\times50}\approx255\ \text{V},\qquad V_{PEAK}\approx361\ \text{V}
$$

$$
I_{RMS}=\sqrt{1300/50}\approx5.1\ \text{A}
$$

An antenna is not always a matched load, so mismatch can make the voltage and current considerably higher. Before drawing the first schematic, we listed the risks that could invalidate a promising simulation:

1. **Isolation:** an OFF semiconductor still has capacitance and can pass RF to the unused output.
2. **Large-signal Coss:** capacitance, charge and voltage sharing change with operating point.
3. **RF layout:** the connector launch, package inductance, copper geometry and chassis return are part of the circuit.
4. **Gate-drive immunity:** high dV/dt can inject Miller current and disturb an OFF gate.
5. **Thermal stress:** conduction loss, switching transients, heatsink insulation and RF heating must be measured together.
6. **Verification:** a control signal is not evidence that the RF branch is actually safe.

These risks define the test sequence: continuity and interlock checks, low-power RF measurements, VNA isolation and insertion-loss measurements, then controlled power increases into a suitable dummy load. An antenna test is a later step, not the first power-up.

## Design 1: the back-to-back MOSFET decision

The first proposal used two identical RF branches. Each branch contains two N-channel SiC MOSFETs connected source-to-source. A single MOSFET is unsuitable for bidirectional RF blocking because its body diode conducts in one direction. With two devices back-to-back, both channels conduct in either RF polarity when ON, while the opposed body diodes block both polarities when OFF.

The complete SP2T power path therefore requires:

$$
2\ \text{branches}\times2\ \text{MOSFETs}=4\ \text{MOSFETs}
$$

This topology was an excellent starting point. It avoided mechanical contacts, offered a clear static ON/OFF state and used widely available high-voltage power components. At this stage, Design 1 looked like a practical route to a solid-state HF switch.

## Why IMZC140R029M2H was selected

The [Infineon IMZC140R029M2H](https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/60/49/infineon-imzc140r029m2h-datasheet-en.pdf) combined the properties we needed for the first design: **1400 V VDS**, low RDS(on), SiC switching behaviour and a TO-247-4 package with a separate Kelvin Source contact.

For the two series devices, the room-temperature channel resistance was estimated at approximately 58 mΩ. The first-order conduction loss at 5.1 A RMS was therefore:

$$
P_{COND}=I_{RMS}^{2}R_{TOTAL}\approx5.1^{2}\times0.058\approx1.5\ \text{W}
$$

That calculation explained the initial confidence in Design 1: voltage margin was comfortable and the calculated ON-state loss was manageable. But RDS(on) only describes the selected branch while it is conducting. The unselected branch is governed by Coss and parasitic coupling.

$$
X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}
$$

At 30 MHz, even a small capacitance can become a significant RF path. A single typical Coss number cannot represent voltage-dependent capacitance, stored charge, floating-node movement and the complete PCB/connector structure.

## The gate-drive chain: UCC21550, bipolar bias and Kelvin Source

The MCU provides a logic command, not the gate current and isolation required by a high-voltage SiC power stage. The [UCC21550ADWR](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/ucc21550.pdf) was chosen for its two independent isolated channels, reinforced isolation, hardware disable, programmable dead time and high source/sink capability.

The MOSFET gate is a charge-driven capacitive input. With approximately 54 nC total gate charge and 14 nC gate-drain charge, the transition current follows the first-order relationship:

$$
I_G\approx Q_G/t_{SW}
$$

The gate resistor, driver impedance and layout inductance therefore have to be tuned together. The objective is controlled switching: a resistor that is too large prolongs the high-loss transition, while a resistor that is too small increases ringing, EMI and oscillation risk.

Each floating gate domain receives an isolated bipolar supply from a [MORNSUN QA053C-1803R3](https://www.mornsun-power.com/public/uploads/pdf/QAxx3C-R3.pdf). The intended states are approximately **+18 V ON** and **−3 to −3.5 V OFF**, measured relative to the MOSFET's own source. The negative rail provides margin against Miller-induced false turn-on:

$$
I_{Miller}=C_{GD}\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}
$$

The Kelvin Source is essential because the power Source path carries RF current. Its parasitic inductance creates a voltage of approximately (V_{LS}=L_S\,di/dt). If the gate return shares that path, the driver no longer controls the true VGS. `Kelvin_A` and `Kelvin_B` keep the gate reference separate from the high-current Source path.

## Control isolation and independent RF confirmation

Enable, branch-select and detector signals are isolated through [TLP291-4 optocouplers](https://toshiba.semicon-storage.com/info/TLP291-4_datasheet_en_20191129.pdf). This blocks uncontrolled ground-loop and common-mode paths from external cables. The isolated signals are combined and conditioned with the [SN74HC08PWR](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/sn74hc08.pdf), creating a hardware gating layer before the isolated driver.

The logic path is not enough by itself. An OFF command only says what the controller requested; it does not prove that RF energy has disappeared from the branch. An independent detector or calibrated power-measurement path must verify the actual RF state before the other branch is enabled:

**Branch OFF → RF level verified low → other branch ON**

The same independent path will be used to distinguish forward power, reflected power, insertion loss and leakage at the unselected port. This separation between command and evidence is essential for trustworthy high-power testing.

## The first review and the decision to redesign

Design 1 was technically coherent and deserved to be built as a baseline. It had a logical RF path, a high-voltage SiC device, isolated bipolar gate drive, Kelvin references, optical control isolation and a staged validation plan.

The first project meeting and design review with Hadrien Theveneau changed the release decision. We concluded that Design 1 should **not be sent to PCB fabrication yet**. The issue was not that the concept was useless; the issue was that the OFF-state isolation margin had not been demonstrated with a large-signal model detailed enough for 30 MHz operation.

The review showed that Coss must be considered together with voltage-dependent charge, floating nodes, connector coupling, PCB parasitics and mismatch conditions. A low RDS(on) result and a favourable nominal Coss value could not, by themselves, guarantee a safe unselected port.

Design 1 therefore became the reference against which the next designs will be judged. We are now comparing series/shunt arrangements, shunt MOSFET options, lower-Coff devices and high-power PIN-diode architectures. The revised schematics and calculations are nearing completion and will appear in the next parts of the series.

This project involves potentially lethal RF voltages and high power. It is not a certified product or a construction recommendation. High-power testing requires suitable loads, shielding, interlocks, calibrated instruments and experienced supervision.
